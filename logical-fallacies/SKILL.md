# Logical fallacies

Review an argument (text, claim, discussion or your own reasoning) for errors of reasoning. Scope is the inference from premises to conclusion, not cognitive biases in general; for statistical errors use the statistical-pitfalls skill.

## Procedure
1. Reconstruct the argument: conclusion, stated premises, and the unstated premises it needs.
2. Read charitably: assess the strongest reasonable version, not a weak paraphrase.
3. For each step, check the list below. Many patterns have legitimate forms (noted in parentheses); name a fallacy only when the inference actually fails in this case.
4. Separate validity (does the conclusion follow?) from soundness (are the premises true?).
5. A fallacious argument does not make its conclusion false (fallacy fallacy); say whether a better argument for it exists.

## Formal (invalid structure)
- Affirming the consequent (if P then Q; Q; so P) and denying the antecedent (if P then Q; not P; so not Q).
- Affirming a disjunct: treating "or" as exclusive (P or Q; P; so not Q).
- Undistributed middle and other invalid syllogisms (all A are C; all B are C; so all A are B).
- Quantifier shift: "everyone has a cause" does not imply "there is one cause of everything".
- Conjunction fallacy: judging "A and B" more probable than A alone.

## Relevance (something other than the argument is addressed)
- Ad hominem: attacking the person, their motives or circumstances, including poisoning the well in advance (legitimate when the person's reliability as a witness is the issue).
- Tu quoque / whataboutism: deflecting with the critic's inconsistency, others' wrongdoing ("two wrongs make a right") or worse problems elsewhere (relative privation).
- Genetic fallacy: judging a claim by its origin rather than its merits, including word origins.
- Appeal to authority: citing someone outside their expertise or against expert consensus (deferring to genuine consensus is legitimate).
- Appeal to popularity, tradition or novelty: true because many believe it, it has always been so, or it is new.
- Appeal to emotion or force: fear, pity, flattery, outrage or threat in place of evidence.
- Appeal to consequences: true or false because the implications are welcome or unwelcome.
- Is–ought and ought–is: deriving what should be from what is (including "natural, so good") or rejecting a fact because it would be undesirable.
- Straw man: refuting a distorted version, including quoting out of context.
- Red herring: shifting to a different issue.

## Presumption (premises assume what is at stake)
- Circular reasoning / begging the question: the conclusion is a premise, sometimes hidden in loaded wording or a persuasive definition.
- Loaded question: a question presupposing an unestablished claim.
- False dilemma: presenting two options when others exist.
- Middle ground: assuming the compromise between two positions is correct.
- Appeal to ignorance and shifted burden: true because not disproven, false because not proven, or "I can't see how it could be" (absence of evidence is evidence only where evidence would be expected).
- Ad hoc rescue: special pleading, redefining terms ("no true Scotsman") or moving the goalposts to avoid counterevidence.
- Unfalsifiable claims: no possible observation could count against them.
- Nirvana fallacy: rejecting a solution because it is imperfect.
- Inconsistency: relying on premises that contradict each other.

## Ambiguity and language
- Equivocation: a term shifting meaning mid-argument, including motte-and-bailey (defending a modest claim, then asserting a bold one).
- Amphiboly: ambiguous grammar exploited.
- Composition and division: inferring properties of the whole from parts or of parts from the whole, including individual-level claims from group data (ecological fallacy).
- Reification: treating an abstraction as a concrete agent or thing.
- Continuum fallacy: rejecting a distinction because the boundary is vague.

## Causation
- Questionable cause: inferring cause from sequence (post hoc) or correlation, ignoring confounders and reverse causation.
- Single cause: attributing an outcome with several causes to one.
- Slippery slope: asserting a chain of consequences without support for each step (legitimate when each step is shown probable).
- Regression fallacy: attributing a return to normal after an extreme to an intervention.
- Gambler's fallacy: expecting independent events to balance out.

## Generalization and evidence
- Hasty generalization: concluding from too few or unrepresentative cases, including anecdotes; and accident, applying a general rule to an exception it does not cover.
- Cherry-picking: citing supporting evidence and ignoring the rest, including survivorship and drawing the target after the shots land (Texas sharpshooter) and judging a group by its worst members.
- Hindsight: judging a past decision by information unavailable when it was made.
- False analogy and false equivalence: inferring from similarity, or treating things as equal, where the relevant features differ.
- Base-rate neglect: ignoring prior probability, including confusing P(evidence | innocent) with P(innocent | evidence) (prosecutor's fallacy).
- McNamara fallacy: counting only what is easily measured.
- Slothful induction: refusing a conclusion strongly supported by evidence.

## Argumentation tactics
- Proof by assertion or repetition, and Gish gallop: many weak claims faster than they can be answered.
- Sunk cost: justifying continuation by past investment rather than future value.

## Output
For each problem: quote the passage, name the fallacy, explain in one or two sentences why the inference fails here, and say what would repair it. Order by impact on the conclusion. End with an overall judgment: does the conclusion still follow once the flaws are removed, and what is the strongest version of the argument?
